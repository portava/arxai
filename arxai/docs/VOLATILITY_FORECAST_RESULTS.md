## Reading these results

**What this measures.** How much a market is going to MOVE over the next 1 hour to 1 day. Not which WAY. These forecasts say nothing about direction, and nothing here is a trading signal on its own.

**What it found.**

- On real FX, gold and stock-index data, volatility is genuinely forecastable. Knowing the hour of the week (the London/New York opens, the Asian lull, index cash sessions) is by far the biggest win. The seasonal models cut forecast loss by a median of about 27% against a flat five-day average, out of sample. That is consistent with the published intraday-volatility literature.
- Volatility clustering on its own (EWMA, HAR without the clock) adds much less at these horizons: a median of 3–8%. Most of what looks like clustering intraday is really the daily cycle.
- The edge shrinks as the horizon grows. At 24 hours ahead it is weaker or not significant for several pairs, because a full day averages over the daily cycle.
- Calibration: the flat baseline's "80%" band actually contains about 83–87% of moves. It is too wide, because it ignores the quiet hours. The seasonal models sit closer to 80%. A few rows sit near 77–79%, slightly too narrow (weekend and overnight gaps are not in the intrabar range proxy).

**Limits.**

- One year of history, which is all Deriv serves: 2025-11-11 → 2026-11-10.
- Deriv's own feed for these markets. Index CFD hours (OTC_*) are not the exchange's.
- The variance proxy is the Parkinson high–low estimator. It misses gaps between bars.
- Not tested: whether better volatility forecasts improve trading P&L. That is the risk-tools lane (vol-scaled sizing and stops), which will be measured separately.

**What it does NOT say.** Nothing here makes price direction predictable. Deriv synthetic indices (Volatility 75 and the like) are excluded: they are random by construction, and their volatility is a fixed parameter.
